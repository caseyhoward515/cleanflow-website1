What Homeowners Usually Notice First

Drainage problems rarely announce themselves as drainage problems. These are the symptoms that bring most people to this page.

The Buried Downspout Backs Up

Water surfaces at the connection during rain instead of disappearing into the ground. The line is accepting less than the roof is sending it, or nothing at all.

Water Spills at the Connection

The downspout and the buried pipe have separated, cracked, or filled in, so water escapes right where the two meet.

Water Pooling Beside the Foundation

Roof runoff is being released too close to the house, or the ground beside it no longer moves water away.

A Soggy, Unusable Part of the Yard

An area stays saturated for days. It may be receiving roof water, catching runoff from higher ground, or sitting where water has nowhere to go.

Runoff Heading the Wrong Direction

Water tracks toward a garage, patio, walkway, or door instead of away from the structures you use.

A Drain That Takes Water Slowly

It works, but not at the rate a real downpour demands. Partial restriction is common and easy to miss until a heavy storm.

Nobody Knows Where the Line Goes

A pipe enters the ground and no one can say where it comes out, whether it still runs anywhere, or if it was ever connected to an outlet.

Sump Discharge Too Close to the House

The pump is doing its job, then releasing the water a few feet from the foundation where it can work its way back down.

The Right Fix Depends on Where the Water Actually Stops

Two properties can show the same puddle for entirely different reasons. One has a downspout line packed with silt; the next has a sound line discharging to a spot that no longer drains. Guessing costs money and often moves the problem rather than solving it, so we work the system in order.

  • Identify the water source — roof runoff, surface runoff, a sump discharge, or ground water moving beneath the surface
  • Inspect and test the existing path with water wherever it is accessible
  • Determine the likely restriction, failure, or simply an outlet that cannot keep up
  • Evaluate the grade and identify realistic discharge locations for the property
  • Recommend the least invasive practical solution that addresses the cause
  • Explain plainly when a repair is enough, when replacement makes more sense, and when a different method belongs in the plan

The correct solution depends on the source of the water, the existing grade, the condition of the current system, and the safest approved discharge point. That is a property-by-property answer, not a package.

Drainage Services and Solutions

These are the tools we draw from. A drainage plan uses the ones the property needs — no property gets the whole list.

Existing Line Testing & Unclogging

We run water into the existing buried line and watch what it does. Where a line is accessible and sound enough to work with, jetting can clear silt, sediment, and root intrusion. Not every buried line can be cleared this way, and we will tell you when we hit that limit rather than keep charging at it.

Underground Downspout Repair & Replacement

Crushed sections, separated joints, collapsed corrugated pipe, and lines that have filled in past recovery. Sometimes a short section is the whole problem; sometimes the run has reached the end of its service life and replacing it costs less than chasing it.

New Solid PVC Downspout Drainage

New underground downspout conveyance built with solid PVC, gradual fittings, and cleanout access, carrying roof water away from the foundation to a discharge point the property can actually support.

Catch Basins & Surface Drains

Where surface water collects in a defined low area, a catch basin gives it a place to enter the system and a sediment sump that can be cleaned out later instead of silting the whole run.

Channel Drains

Suited to hard surfaces — a driveway apron, garage entry, patio edge, or walkway — where water runs as a sheet across pavement and needs intercepting along a line rather than at a single point.

French Drains, Where They Fit

A French drain collects water moving through the soil. That makes it the right answer for subsurface and ground water problems and the wrong answer for a clogged downspout line. We use them where the water is actually in the ground, not as a default.

Grading & Water Channeling

Sometimes the most durable fix is shaping the ground so water leaves on its own. Practical regrading and channeling can reduce how much has to be piped in the first place.

Dedicated Sump Discharge

A sump pump needs its own route to its own outlet, sized and placed so the water it removes does not simply return. This is always planned as a separate line.

Pop-Up Emitters & Dry Wells

A pop-up emitter releases water at a chosen point away from the home and closes back down between rains. A dry well holds volume and lets it soak away — useful where soil and site conditions allow it, and unsuitable where they do not.

Sidewalk Boring & Concrete Access

Where a line has to cross a sidewalk or slab, boring underneath usually avoids cutting it. When concrete access genuinely cannot be avoided, we say so before the work starts and handle the excavation and restoration.

Approved Discharge Connections

Some properties have a roadside ditch, curb, catch basin, or municipal outlet available. Those connections are possible where an approved outlet exists and when permitted — which is a question we settle before promising a route, not after.

Backfill & Landscape Restoration

Turf and plantings are protected where practical, trenches are backfilled and compacted, and the work area is restored to a reasonable condition rather than left as a scar across the yard.

Sump Discharge Is Always Its Own Route

A sump pump can move a large volume of water in a short burst, and it runs on its own schedule rather than the weather's. Tying that discharge into an existing underground downspout line means the two compete for the same pipe at the worst possible moment — and a restriction anywhere in that shared run sends water back toward the house.

CleanFlow evaluates sump discharge as a separate, dedicated route with its own outlet. We do not recommend connecting a sump-pump discharge into an existing underground downspout line.

How we build it

Built for Flow and Future Access

Most of the failed underground downspout lines we dig up were not badly damaged. They were built in a way that made them impossible to maintain and easy to block. Where site conditions and project scope allow, this is how CleanFlow builds instead.

  • ASTM 2729 solid PVC for underground downspout conveyance — smooth wall, so silt and debris keep moving instead of settling into ridges
  • No corrugated pipe on new solid downspout-conveyance runs. Corrugation traps sediment, crushes under load, and is the most common thing we replace
  • Gradual 45-degree fittings underground rather than sharp 90-degree turns, so debris has a path through the bend instead of a wall to pack against
  • Wye cleanouts with removable plugs at downspout transitions, so the line can be opened and cleared later without excavation
  • Roughly 1 to 2 percent slope where site conditions permit — enough fall to keep solids moving with the water
  • Accessible maintenance points placed where they can practically be reached, not buried and forgotten
  • A discharge point chosen for the property and an approved outlet, rather than the nearest convenient spot
  • Landscape protection, proper backfill, and reasonable restoration as part of the job rather than an afterthought
  • Follow-up consideration for normal soil settling where that is included in the project scope

Depth, slope, and outlet are decided by the property. Utility locations, existing structures, frost considerations, tree roots, and where the water can legally and practically go all shape the final design, so we do not publish a single number and apply it everywhere.

What to Expect

Drainage projects vary widely in scope, so the timeline does too. The sequence does not.

1. Assessment

We walk the property, look at the roof and the ground together, test what is accessible with water, and find out what happens during real rain.

2. Findings & Proposed Solution

You hear what we found, what we think is causing it, and which approach we recommend — including the options we considered and set aside, and why.

3. Clear Written Estimate

Scope, materials, and price in writing before anything is dug. Where approvals, permits, or utility locating apply, that is identified up front.

4. Installation or Repair

The work itself, built to the standards above, with landscaping protected where practical and the site kept as contained as the job allows.

5. Flow Verification

Water goes through the finished system while we watch it. A drainage line is not finished because it is buried; it is finished because it moves water.

6. Cleanup & Documentation

Backfill, restoration, and photos of what was found and what was built — including where the cleanouts are, so the next person is not guessing.

Drainage pricing is property-specific. Trench length, depth, surface type, access, restoration, and the available outlet all change the number, so we price the project in front of us rather than publishing a rate that would not apply to your home.
